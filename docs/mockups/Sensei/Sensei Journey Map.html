01

Executive summary

要

Sensei's individual-developer experience is in strong shape: the team has repeatedly chosen value before setup and one decision, one default, and most historical friction has been resolved. What remains splits cleanly — a short list of operational issues (one genuinely critical), a recurring discoverability-of-depth weakness, and a single strategic theme that recurs in four places: the product is built for one developer talking to one global community, with nothing modelled for the company in between.

結
Headline gap · closed this pass

The organization sat between the individual and the global collective — and now it's wired in.

A developer's hard-won patterns, guards and personas can only stay local or broadcast to the world — there is no company-scoped destination, no team review of what leaves a machine, and no governed way to push approved practice back down to everyone. For individuals this is invisible; for any organization adopting Sensei it is the blocker. The fix is a distinct module — Dōjō, a company-hosted hive-mind — specified in §3.

3.1

The shape — priority ladders, not a hierarchy

Knowledge isn't a clean org tree — teams hold competing principles, and the same decision can be argued two ways. So Dōjō ranks rather than nests: each concern is a ladder of principles ordered by priority and impact, and when two collide the higher rung wins. Scope — company · team · project · repo · stack — rides along as a tag on every rung, not as the structure.

When principles compete, priority & impact resolve it — the higher rung wins, regardless of which scope authored it.
3.2

Memberships & routing — one developer, many orgs

A developer rarely belongs to just one org. An indie joins communities; an employee has a company; a consultant carries their employer and a client org per engagement — sometimes all at once. Dōjō models this as multiple memberships, and every project is bound to exactly one of them, so findings route only where they belong and never pollute an unrelated hive-mind.

客
Client takes precedence. When a project belongs to a client engagement, the client org's rules and confidentiality win over the employer's — what's shared, and how it's attributed, follow the client's policy first.
3.3

Attribution & confidentiality — who gets credit, what gets stripped

How a contribution is credited depends on where it came from and where it's going. Personal open-source work carries your name; closed-source and employer work stay named but internal; client work is automatically dereferenced — the learning, its cause and context travel, but the source reference is dropped, so the lesson can be shared anywhere without leaking.

3.5

The lifecycle — contribute → accumulate → triage → approve → distribute

A one-way pipeline upstream, then a governed return downstream. Nothing is published org-wide without passing triage and a named approval; once approved, it lands automatically in every matching scope.

↑ distributed back · downstream to every matching scope
3.6

Auth, roles & controls

Even self-hosted, a Dōjō is never open access. Every connection authenticates against identity the org already runs, and roles are derived from the git provider, then fine-tuned — so a new member is productive immediately without a manual setup step.

Authentication

No anonymous reads or writes. A connection proves who you are through an identity source the org already operates.

鍵
No open access. Membership and scope are derived from verified access — never assumed from being on the network, even internally.
Role provisioning · derive, then fine-tune

Dōjō seeds each member from their git roles, taking the highest role across their associated repos as a safe default. Admins then fine-tune — adding the roles git doesn't model and overriding per project.

Auto-provisioned at the derived role on first connect (the fast path) · Dōjō-only roles — reviewer / approver, admin, developer, tester — and per-project overrides layer on top, since git models none of them and they vary by project.

3.7

Global Collective vs. Dōjō

They coexist. The Collective is the public commons; Dōjō is the private, governed one. A contribution can go to either — or be promoted from the company up to the world, once it's been generalised.
